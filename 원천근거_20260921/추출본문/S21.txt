Abstract
The primary aim of this systematic review was to examine the efficacy of driving interventions with regard to a reduction in motor vehicle crashes and improvements in driving skills among older people. The secondary aim was to identify the optimal type (on‐road or off‐road) and dosage (period, sessions, and duration) of driving interventions for improving driving skills in older people. We searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, and Scopus of Systematic Reviews for papers published from their inception to December 1, 2020, as well as the reference lists of the included papers. The selected studies were randomized controlled trials examining the effects of driving interventions among community‐dwelling older drivers aged 65 years and over. A meta‐analysis of two studies (
n
= 960) showed that driving interventions significantly reduced the number of motor vehicle crashes per person‐years. Ten studies (
n
= 575) were included in the meta‐analysis showing that the interventions significantly improved the driving skill scores. Driving skill scores significantly improved after on‐road training, and in interventions of at least 3 h, 3 sessions, and 3 weeks. Driving interventions significantly improve driving skills and reduce motor vehicle crashes among older drivers aged 65 years and over. On‐road training is more efficacious than off‐road training and driving interventions of at least 3 h taking place in 3 sessions over a period of 3 weeks may be required to improve driving skills in older drivers.

Keywords:
car crash, driving skill, driving training, meta‐analysis, older adults
This systematic review and meta‐analysis provides the first synthesized evidence demonstrating the efficacy of driving interventions in improving driving skills and reducing motor vehicle crashes among older adult drivers aged 65 years and over. Moreover, the subgroup analysis of intervention type indicates that on‐road training is more efficacious than off‐road training. Regarding intervention dosage, our findings implied that driving interventions of at least 3 h taking place in 3 sessions over a period of 3 weeks may be required to improve older adults' driving skills.
Introduction
The global population is aging, and the number of people aged 65 years or older is projected to increase from 727 million in 2020 to over 1.5 billion in 2050. In Japan, one in three (5.13 million) people aged 75 years or older held a driving license in 2016.
1
Not only is driving a car essential for transportation but it also allows older adults to maintain their life‐space mobility,
2
and it can impact their health.
3
For example, previous studies have reported that driving cessation could lead to frailty, disability, and even mortality.
4
,
5
,
6
However, the rate of fatal accidents caused by older drivers is considerably higher (8.9 fatal accidents per 100 000 in license holders ≥75 years) than that caused by younger drivers (< 75 years: 3.8).
1
Moreover, the proportion of fatal accidents caused by older drivers has doubled in the past decade in Japan (7.4% in 2006 to 13.5% in 2016).
1
Thus, effective measures to reduce traffic accidents caused by older drivers while allowing them to continue driving are required.
Studies have reported various intervention programs to address age‐related declines in sensory, cognitive, and physical functions that aim to improve driving skills and reduce traffic accidents caused by older adults.

However, as the abovementioned review included middle‐aged adults (aged ≥50 years), the results may not be generalizable to older adults, who are generally defined as individuals aged 65 years and older. Furthermore, optimal driving training methods (e.g., driving simulator or on‐road training) and dosage (e.g., number of sessions), which are critical to the development of training programs, remain unclear. Therefore, the aim of this study was to systematically review and statistically synthesize available evidence regarding the efficacy of driving interventions with regard to reductions in motor vehicle crashes and improvements in driving skills among older adults. The secondary aim was to identify the optimal type (on‐road or off‐road) and dosage (period, sessions, and duration) of driving interventions to improve driving skills in older adults.
Methods
Registry of this systematic review protocol
This review complied with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines.
24
We prospectively registered the study protocol with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD42021227676).
Literature search strategy
We searched four electronic databases (MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, and Scopus) for papers published from their inception to December 1, 2020. We developed the search strategy based on previous systematic reviews on driving interventions and adapted it to include only randomized controlled trials (RCTs), refined as follows: “(safety OR education OR training OR driving OR simulator OR program OR countermeasures) AND (“older driver*” OR “senior driver*” OR “aged driver*” OR “elderly driver*” OR “old driver*”) AND (random* or “Randomized Controlled Trial”[pt]).” We did not apply any language or other restrictions to the initial search. Additionally, we searched the reference lists of the included studies for relevant papers.
Eligibility criteria
Eligible studies were RCTs investigating the effects of any driving training programs including on‐road, education, cognitive, and virtual training on incidence of car crash and/or driving skill assessment scores. The target population was community‐dwelling older adults aged 65 years and above. We excluded studies investigating disease‐specific samples (e.g. Parkinson's disease, stroke). The studies had to have no intervention controls or active controls that could appropriately investigate the effects of driving interventions. We excluded study protocols, abstracts, and papers published in languages other than English.
Screening process and data extraction
Two independent teams of reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of studies to identify those that potentially met the inclusion criteria. The review teams then retrieved the full texts of these studies and independently assessed them for eligibility. To avoid missing potentially eligible studies reporting relevant outcomes only in the full text, we did not apply the outcome criterion during the title and abstract screening. The teams resolved any disagreements over the eligibility of studies through discussion with a third reviewer.
Next, we used a standardized, pre‐piloted form to extract data from the included studies for assessment of study quality and evidence synthesis. The extracted information included study setting; study population, participant demographics, and baseline characteristics; details of the intervention and control conditions; outcomes and length of follow‐up; and information for assessment of the risk of bias. Two review authors independently extracted the data and resolved any disagreements through discussion with a third review author. Thereafter, the present authors contacted the authors of the selected studies to request missing data related to study outcomes and eligibility.
Risk of bias assessment
Using the Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) scale, two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias of the included studies. This instrument consists of 11 items related to internal and external validity as well as to statistical information necessary for the interpretation of data. We allotted one point for each fulfilled criterion (maximum 11 points) and resolved disagreements through discussion and with the involvement of a third reviewer.
Statistical analyses
Analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan, Version 5.4; The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark). We pooled the studies that used the same outcome measures using random‐effects meta‐analyses. Next, we calculated the standardized mean differences (SMDs) for continuous outcomes with different measurement units and the rate ratios (RRs) for the rate of car crashes with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two‐sided
P
‐values. For the synthesis of RRs, we used the generic inverse variance method, with the natural logarithm and standard error of the RR entered into the analysis. We conducted preplanned and post‐hoc subgroup analyses to examine possible differential intervention effects related to intervention type (on‐road training vs. off‐road training) and dosage (period, sessions, and duration). Furthermore, we performed a preplanned sensitivity analysis based on study quality and assessed publication bias by examining funnel plots and excluding asymmetric studies, considering
P
‐values less than 0.05 as statistically significant.

Discussion
To the best of our knowledge, this systematic review and meta‐analysis provides the first synthesized evidence demonstrating the efficacy of driving interventions in improving driving skills and reducing motor vehicle crashes among older drivers aged 65 years and over. The subgroup analysis on intervention type indicates that on‐road training is more efficacious than off‐road training. With regard to intervention dosage, our findings imply that driving interventions of at least 3 h taking place in 3 sessions over a period of 3 weeks may be required to improve driving skills in older drivers.
Our meta‐analysis provides evidence that driving interventions significantly reduce the rate of motor vehicle crashes caused by older drivers per person‐year by 32%. This is in line with the previous systematic review and meta‐analysis by Fausto
et al
. reporting a 27% reduction in at‐fault crashes among subjects aged 50 years and older.
23
This suggests that individually tailored driving lessons can successfully address poor driving skills and unsafe driving habits that could lead to car crashes in older drivers aged 65 or older.

,
12
,

,
16
,

The reduction in real‐life motor vehicle crashes implies a meaningful generalization and retention of the safe driving skills that older drivers learned during the driving interventions.

17
Although traffic regulations and required training content may vary between countries, on‐road training individually supervised by an instructor may be an important element of effective driving skill intervention programs for older adults.
With regard to dosage, our subgroup analyses (Fig.
5
) suggested that at least 3 h taking place in 3 sessions over a period of 3 weeks of driving intervention are required to improve driving skills in older drivers. This required training dosage, not reported previously,
23
has an important implication about the key training program components. Tailored interventions based on the driving characteristics of older adults take a certain amount of time to cover important areas such as basic handling, blind spot checks, passing railroad crossings, and hazard anticipation. All six studies involving on‐road driving training were conducted alongside an educational program,
7
,
9
,
12
,
14
,
16
,
17
which takes at least a few hours. Generally, multiple on‐road driving sessions are required to evaluate individual driving behaviors, provide feedback and ensure sufficient practice and retention of skills, although some individuals may need more sessions than others. Thus, the minimum dosage of driving interventions should be 3 h taking place in 3 sessions over a period of 3 weeks.
The overall quality of the included studies was acceptable to good, with the PEDro scores ranging from 5 to 9. Owing to the nature of the driving interventions, blinding of subjects and therapists was not possible. Thus, the highest PEDro score was 9. Concealed allocation (27%), baseline comparability (55%), and intention‐to‐treat analysis (45%) were not confirmed in some included studies. Thus, future studies should meet these quality indicators to minimize the potential risk of bias. While no visual indication of potential publication bias was found from the funnel plot, studies on driving interventions are evolving, and updates to this review by way of more studies will be necessary.
In Japan, drivers aged 75 years or older are obligated to take part in cognitive screenings at the time of license renewal (those with a history of certain offenses have also been obliged to undertake a driving skill assessment since May 2022). While older adults with a confirmed diagnosis of dementia are disqualified from license renewal, those who renew their driving licenses are provided with refresher training. However, the refresher training is currently limited to only one session at the first license renewal and no associated significant decrease in the rate of traffic accidents among drivers aged 75 years or over has been shown.
28
Similarly in Denmark, screening for cognitive impairment on its own in older drivers had no effect on safety.
29
Therefore, refresher training with on‐road training, which is conducted for at least 3 h taking place in 3 sessions over a period of 3 weeks, may be necessary to reduce the number of accidents at the national level. However, the efficacy of such interventions must be investigated in future research.
Although we followed the PRISMA guidelines, including prospective registration, an exhaustive literature search, and acquisition of unpublished data, for our systematic review and meta‐analysis, we acknowledge several limitations. First, the meta‐analysis included only 11 papers; future meta‐analyses should be conducted with more driving intervention studies among older adults. Second, we may have omitted relevant papers not published in English. Third, we assessed driving skills in various forms, including on‐road
7
,
9
,
12
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
and driving simulator training,
10
,
18
without standardization. In addition, the results of on‐road training differ depending on whether evaluation in unsafe driving actions and their severity (e.g. starting, stopping, and moving in roadway) was conducted for the entire predetermined course
7
,
12
,
14
,
16
,
17
or for specific situations.
9
Therefore, it is necessary to unify the evaluation method. Fourth, we used driving skill data post‐intervention, and thus did not assess mid‐ to long‐term retention in this review.
This systematic review and meta‐analysis of 11 studies demonstrated that the efficacy of driving interventions in improving driving skills and reducing motor vehicle crashes among older drivers aged 65 years and over. The subgroup analysis on intervention type indicates that on‐road training is more efficacious than off‐road training. With regard to intervention dosage, our findings implied that driving interventions of at least 3 h taking place in 3 sessions over a period of 3 weeks may be required to improve driving skills in older drivers.
