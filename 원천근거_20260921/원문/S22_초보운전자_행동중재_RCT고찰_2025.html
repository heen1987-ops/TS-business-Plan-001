The effectiveness of behavioural interventions on young, novice drivers’ motor vehicle crash risk: A systematic review

Globally, young drivers account for a small percentage of licenced drivers but are over-represented in fatal and serious motor vehicle crashes (MVCs).1–7 For example, in 2019 young drivers were involved in 16% of all fatal and serious crashes on UK roads, despite accounting for only 7% of all licenced drivers.5 In 2020, young drivers aged 16–19 years-old in the USA had a crash rate per mile driven four times higher than drivers aged 20 years and above.2 Across the European Union 42% of fatalities among 18–24 year-olds were due to MVCs in 2019.7 Policy-level interventions, such as Graduated Driver Licensing (GDL) regulations, are the most effective intervention available to decrease young drivers’ risk for MVCs.8 GDL works by reducing exposure to risky contexts and situations for newly licensed young drivers.9

Thus far, there is no analogous individual-level intervention or set of intervention approaches that has achieved what GDL has accomplished. Nevertheless, investigations of individual-level risk factors and interventions continue. Commonly studied individual-level factors include developmental characteristics (e.g., neurodevelopmental), individual differences (e.g., personality, health), driving-related skill and experience, and the quality of interpersonal relationships with important others (e.g., parents, peers).10–12 Despite decades of research examining how these factors contribute to risky driving behaviour, there is no consensus on how to best intervene on them.13

To address this issue, the purpose of this systematic review was to evaluate the evidence concerning the effectiveness of individual-level behavioural interventions. We were interested only in studies that used formal randomised controlled trials (RCT) designs and that reported post-license real-world crashes. RCTs are the gold-standard approach for identifying interventions and treatments for a range of injuries and diseases. RCTs enable causal inferences because these experimental designs ensure that a) the exposure to the intervention / independent variable precedes the measurement of the outcome / dependent variable in time (i.e., they establish temporal precedence), b) there is a quantification of any statistical association between the intervention and the outcome of interest / dependent variable (i.e., they evaluate strength of covariation), and c) they limit selection bias by randomly assigning intervention conditions to people (i.e., they provide strong internal validity). Prior systematic reviews on injury prevention interventions in paediatric populations have also focused on RCTs for this reason.14,15 In sum, RCTs are the best research design for making causal inferences.16 However, poorly designed or executed trials can lead to biased estimates of effect and ultimately suboptimal clinical and public health decision-making.17 RCTs require planning, regulatory and reporting responsibility, funding, time, access to resources, advanced research training, and enough statistical power.18 They also require research evidence strong enough to inform interventions worthy of trialling.

The quality of research evidence (in any field) can be compromised by overly flexible and undisclosed data management, analytic, and reporting practices that increase the risk for publishing false positive results.19,20 These unreported “researcher degrees of freedom” include hypothesizing after the results are known (HARKing) and manipulating data and re-running significance tests until a significant result is obtained (P-value hacking).19 In response to growing concerns about a rigour and reproducibility crisis affecting multiple domains of science, Bishop (2019) identified the “four horsemen of irreproducibility” as publication bias, low statistical power, P-value hacking, and HARKing.21 As with other fields, these issues could be contributing to a noisy evidence-base making it difficult to design effective interventions for young drivers.

Method

We systematically evaluated the literature on the effectiveness of individual-level behavioural interventions on reducing young drivers’ MVC risk. Studies needed to have: 1) random assignment of intervention conditions to people, 2) individual-level behavioural interventions (e.g., any psycho-behavioural, educational, or psychosocial intervention) delivered in any intervention modality, 3) target young drivers 16–24 years of age, young drivers and their carer (dyads), or carers of young drivers, and 4) collect and report young drivers’ post-license MVC outcomes. Surrogate measures for MVCs (e.g., near crashes) were not accepted as equivalent to MVCs for this purpose. Studies were excluded if they were not written in English, had a non-experimental design or did not use random assignment, did not assess young drivers’ post-license MVCs, or included children and adolescents (≤15 years of age) or only targeted drivers over 25 years-old.

Fourteen databases were searched from May-to-June 2023 (e.g., Scopus, PubMed, MEDLINE, EBSCOhost, TRID). The types of sources searched included peer-reviewed journals, academic databases (e.g., Thesis and Dissertations), Industry, and Government reports (RAC Foundation). Dates of publication ran from 2000 - May/June 2023. The search was re-run to include studies published between July 2023 and April 2024, but no new studies were identified. Covidence software supported screening and data extraction using pre-determined categories.22 Data were extracted for each paper by two of the following three authors (J.F., A.A, H.M.) and checked by a third (J.H. or CCM). The Risk-of-Bias 2 (RoB2) tool was used to assess the quality of each eligible study across five domains: (1) the randomization process, (2) deviations from intended interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) measurement of the outcome, and (5) selection of the reported result. RoB2 assessment tool is recommended by Cochrane for randomised controlled trials.23 Two raters (J.F., A.A, H.M.) independently evaluated each paper and discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third rater (JH or CCM).

Our focus was on MVCs but most studies also collected other outcome data (e.g., near crashes, tickets, attitudes etc). Our assessment was restricted to MVCs and does not reflect information related to other endpoints; this is relevant for domains 3 (missing outcome data) and 4 (measurement of the outcome). Figure 1 depicts the PRISMA diagram illustrating 16 evaluable papers. Table 1 contains summary data for these papers which corresponded to 12 trials and includes the quality assessment. We were unable to use a consistent reporting method owing to the significant heterogeneity in how analyses were conducted and reported in the original papers. We note that the Driver2020 project was a stratified randomized control trial of 5 different individual-level behavioural interventions conducted in the UK, but neither the evaluation abstract nor report were publicly available, only the trial registration, thus we could not include it in our Results.24 Additionally, if test-statistics and exact p-values were not reported originally, we were unable to include them here. In keeping with best-practices in open science and rigor and reproducibility, the protocol was pre-registered with Prospero (CRD42023425135). The full systematic review protocol is available at: https://tinyurl.com/yc5kxbh8.

Results

We organised the results section to correspond with the common categories or types of interventions we observed: Interventions targeting young drivers with ADHD, parent-directed interventions, and driver education (classroom, behind-the-wheel, or simulator training).

Interventions for youth with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)

In Ohio, the computer-based Focused Concentration and Attention Learning (FOCAL) program targeted long-glances away from the roadway among licensed young drivers diagnosed with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD).25 The study’s primary endpoints were long-glances and lane deviation and crashes or near crashes as evaluated by in-vehicle data recordings over 12-month period after training. There were 71 teen drivers in the intervention group with evaluable in-vehicle data recorded and 70 in the control group (over 90% of the original sample). The rate of collision or near-collision per g-force event at the group-level was 3.4% and 5.6%, respectively (relative risk, 0.60, 95% CI, 0.41 to 0.89). Crashes were not reported separately, and event rates were not calculated at the individual-level, thus the impact of the intervention on MVCs cannot be ascertained. There were 110 “events” out of 3,213 total recorded event units in the intervention group of 71 teens and 159 “events” out of 2,818 total recorded event units in the control group of 70 teens, thus there are almost twice as many more “events” (269) than people (141) in the study. In the original submission to ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02848092), it appears the study was powered for between-group analyses of the long-glance data, there was initially an intention for the in-vehicle data to be counts and not rates, to request driving records from the Bureau of Motor Vehicles for the 12-months of post-intervention driving, and to count the number of MVCs, but these analyses were not reported in the published manuscript.

In New York, the Supporting the Effective Entry to the Roadway (STEER) program for learner teen drivers diagnosed with ADHD-combined-type was an 8-week program targeting behavioural management and teen communication training along with in-vehicle monitoring and simulator training (n=172 teenagers equally allocated).26 The control group was driving education and driving practice for 10 weeks consisting of both in-class and on-road components. This study was powered for antecedent psychological and behavioural outcomes and was primarily concerned with the relationship between stimulant medication and intervention effectiveness in addition to the main effect analyses. Crash involvement data were collected through teen self-report, parent report, or department of motor vehicles records at the end of treatment and 6- and 12-month follow-up points. Behavioural data were also collected using in-vehicle monitoring devices for 4 weeks following each assessment point. No significant difference in MVCs were found between the two groups; however, MVCs were combined with tickets, so it is not possible to report or evaluate an effect on crashes alone.

Parent-directed interventions

In Connecticut, the Checkpoints program was evaluated in a state-wide randomised controlled trial of n=4,295 young drivers and their parents, the results of which were published in three papers.27–29 The Checkpoints Program consisted of a videotape, 8 mailed newsletters, and a parent-teen contract to facilitate family-based driving limits (e.g., house rules) on risky driving behaviours and the control group received generic received print-based information about driver safety on the same schedule as the intervention group all timed to the receipt of the learner’s permit. All three papers, published in 2006, did not report a priori power calculations. Self-reported crash involvement was collected through phone surveys at 3-months post, 6-months post, and 12-months post license. There were no between group differences in crash involvement observed. The number of self-reported MVCs were identical between the two groups at 6 months M(SD)=.33(.60) and highly similar at 12 months: M(SD)=.47(.71) for the intervention group and M(SD)=.48(.72) for the control group. The risk of bias assessment noted concerns about self-report bias and missing data (e.g., data from 1,483 young people were classed as “missing”) but no strong evidence that this would have affected between group comparisons.

In Michigan, Checkpoints was adapted for use in classroom settings in groups of teens enrolled in driver education and their parents. To evaluate this adapted model of delivery a group randomized controlled trial was conducted with 8 instructors from different driving schools randomly assigned to intervention or control groups.30 These instructors then recruited teens and their parents from their schools/classes resulting in 148 students and one parent (99 intervention; 49 control group) being enrolled in the study. A noteworthy change from the home-based model was the active role that instructors had in facilitating completion of the parent-teen agreement which is at the heart of the program and it was the intention that families left the session with a completed agreement. Despite this, and similar to the home-based version of Checkpoints, there was no effect on crashes 6 months after the intervention was completed. Self-reported crash involvement was intervention = 12% and control = 9%, which was reported as not significant.

In Iowa, the Steering Teens Safe (STS) program was evaluated in a three-arm RCT comparing its effectiveness alongside feedback from an in-vehicle driver video recording (IVDR) system, an IVDR and feedback system alone, and a control group with just the in-vehicle recording system, but no feedback, for 4 weeks among soon to be licensed young drivers and their parents.31,32 An IVDR risky event rate was the dependent variable of interest which was derived by taking the number of events divided by miles driven (rate of driving events per 1000 miles) for 4 months post intervention and comparing this rate between the groups. There were no reported power calculations. There were no observed significant differences between the Mean(SD) of crashes/near crashes per 1,000 miles for the control group: 1.3 (3.0); event recorder with feedback group: 1.0 (2.0); and event recorder with feedback and STS 0.7 (2.0) groups; all comparisons were p > .05. Crashes were combined with near crashes, so it is not possible to evaluate an effect on crashes alone.

In Pennsylvania, the TeenDrivingPlan (TDP) intervention program33,34 was evaluated in a randomized controlled trial of 512 learner teen drivers and their parents with an allocation ratio of 3:2, intervention to control (usual care condition) in Pennsylvania. The TDP was a web-based practice driving support tool for families of learner teen drivers consisting of instructional practice videos, a practice planner, and an interactive driving log and practice self-rating tool. MVC data from the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation were linked with driver license numbers for 4 years post intervention. Results indicated no significant associations between exposure to TDP and a reduction in crash risk. The relative reduction in risk was: adjusted hazard ratio = .80 (95% confidence interval [CI] .44, 1.43) and the unadjusted absolute risk reduction was 1.1% (95% CI −4.4, 7.1).35 There was a low risk of bias in this study, but there was a lack of statistical power. No a priori power calculations were reported for the crash outcome. Note two of the authors[JH, DLL] were involved in analyses of the TDP trial data and JH co-led the TDP trial.

In Michigan, an in-vehicle driver recording device (IVDR) RCT was conducted with n=90 parent-teen dyads. The trial evaluated the comparative effectiveness of a “lights only” intervention consisting of providing real-time IVDR feedback to teens with a “lights-plus” intervention consisting of providing both real-time feedback and also providing families access to the IVDR’s recorded driving events and placement of the teen’s driving performance in comparison with other same-aged licensed teen drivers. There were no reported power calculations. After 15-weeks of IVDR monitoring in each condition there was no detectable effect on crashes. There were three at-fault crash events recorded for the lights plus group compared with 11 at-fault crash events for the lights only group; p>.05. Similar to the TDP study, there was a low risk of bias but there was likely a lack of statistical power.36

Driver education: classroom, behind-the-wheel, or simulator training.

In Montana, a defensive driving program consisting of nine hours of classroom and behind-the-wheel instruction delivered in one day followed by an individualized report card and associated driving practice homework was evaluated in comparison to a control group of teens in a RCT with n=347 licensed teen drivers recruited from high school driver education courses. Self-reported crash involvement was collected annually for 4 years and Department of Motor Vehicle crash records were obtained where possible. There were no reported power calculations. The number of reported collisions were adjusted per the number of hours driven per month and evaluated for between-group differences. No significant differences were found in adjusted models, test-statistics and rates are not presented in the report, only the unadjusted comparison results were provided.37 This study was determined to have a high degree of a bias owing to a lack of detail about the randomization process, attrition, unclear results reporting, and also potentially was under-powered. This trial was published in a report and not a peer-reviewed journal so part of the difficulty with data extraction came from the length and organization of a long-form government report as opposed to what one would typically find in a (relatively) more succinct journal format.

In North Carolina, an Advanced Driver Training Program (ADTP) lasting 4.5 – 5 hours spread across classroom and experiential (e.g., skid control) behind-the-wheel and simulation (e.g., drunk goggles) activities targeting adjudicated offending young drivers was evaluated in a RCT with (n=599) participants. The trial compared the ADTP to traditional driver education program, held in person for roughly the same amount of time. Random assignment was based on birth month and control participants were allowed to choose which of two court-approved educational programs they wanted to attend. There were no reported power calculations. Police-reported crash data were linked with administrative records of the drivers for ~ 550 days post intervention. There was no detectable significant difference in time to first crash by intervention status: (χ2 (2) = 3.36, p = .18).38 The risk of bias analysis identified some concerns, as there was some slippage of exposures (control participants taking online courses) coupled with intervention participants not-completing ADTP, although this was adjusted for in the analysis. More detail about the randomisation process would have also been helpful to understand how birth month was used to assign participants.

In Ohio, a pilot n=40 simulator-based training program was evaluated in an experimental design whereby teen licensed drivers (had to be licensed for < 12 months and be citation and crash free) were recruited from a high school and then randomly assigned to participate in the 12-session training in a driving simulator or to a control group. The simulator sessions were intended to be progressively more challenging requiring a 70% score to pass in order to take the next module. Each session included a quiz, practice drive, and then an assessment drive. Crash involvement was ascertained by obtaining driving records for the 18 months following study completion from the Ohio Department of Public Safety’s Bureau of Motor Vehicles database. Analyses of these records indicated that 6.2% of the intervention group was involved in a crash compared with 21.1% of the control group (p= 0.35), which was not statistically significant.39 The risk of bias analysis yielded significant concerns including high attrition because not enough teens passed through all 12 modules, thus necessitating an as-treated analysis of only half of the study participants. However, this was a pilot study and its purpose was to evaluate feasibility of the intervention.

In Connecticut, a randomised trial of the StreetReady simulator education program was completed with n=215 teenagers recruited from a public high school of unclear license status with 126 assigned to the intervention group and 86 to the control group. The allocation ratio was not specified but designed to over-allocate to the intervention group with the expectation the program would be difficult to complete. The program consisted of 12-lessons between 20–30 minutes long entailing a tutorial, practice drive, and assessment drive. A score of 70% was required to progress to the next lesson. Students had 6 months to complete these lessons during their free periods at their school. Participants were asked to complete a telephone survey between 6–12 months following their last lesson indicating their crash involvement. There were no reported power calculations. Six out of 50 (12%) licensed control group teens reported a crash compared with 4 out of 27 (15%) of the licensed intervention group teens who completed all the lessons of simulator training, p > .05. The risk of bias assessment revealed some concerns, namely poor compliance with the intervention coupled with high attrition. Only 69% of the intervention group completed the pre- and postsurvey and obtained a driver’s license compared with 56% of the control group.40 This study is also likely to be under-powered.

In Australia, a randomised controlled trial of the P Drivers Programme41 was conducted with recently licensed (within 100 days) teen drivers (n = 35,109). The Program lasted 3–6 weeks and was psychoeducational in nature and designed to address multiple behavioural targets (e.g., speed choice, hazard perception), attitudes, and beliefs. This program had several phased components including a self-assessment survey, 3-hour group session on risky driving with a group of teen young drivers and facilitator, an on-road coaching session with a facilitator and peer passengers, and a second 3-hour group session focusing on goals for safe driving. Maintenance messages were sent via email and text messages to some participants to encourage exposure and adherence to the program. The main endpoints were self-reported and police-reported motor vehicle crashes. Self-reported crash involvement was 21.2% in the intervention group and 21.7% in the control group: HR(95%CI) 0.98 (0.84–1.07). Police-reported crashes were 0.6% in the intervention group and 0.7% in the control group; HR(95%CI) 1.10 (0.31–3.86).42 The risk of bias analysis indicated that the study had significant attrition, had to change the allocation ratio to adjust for this problem after study start, and move to an as-treated analysis owing to extremely poor uptake of the intervention components. A process evaluation indicated that lack of interest, not enough time, and complexity of the program were key factors in the high attrition rate.

The Alcohol Misuse Prevention Study (AMPS) was a high school-based alcohol prevention program for Michigan students in the 10th grade during 1988–89 and 1989–90.43 It consisted of 5, 45 minute classroom sessions focused on preventing alcohol use. AMPS was compared with a usual care condition in a randomized controlled trial with (n=4,635) participants. The program targeted students’ awareness of the short-term effects of alcohol, risks of alcohol misuse, and contextual factors that could lead to taking risks with alcohol. A follow-up study was conducted to determine the program’s effect on police-reported at-fault crashes. No effect on crashes was identified. Relative risk rates were unadjusted: 0.89 (0.72–1.10) and adjusted:0.93 (0.74–1.16) for the first year of follow-up. The risk of bias analysis yielded no significant concerns. The analyses were limited to young people who took a pre-test but there was no indication that this choice affected between group comparisons.

Discussion

We identified many well-intentioned studies designed to reduce the significant problem of morbidity and mortality among young people by reducing their risk for MVCs. All of the papers we reviewed reported results from trials that evaluated more proximal outcome variables addition to MVCs (e.g., risky driving) and most reported positive results. Nevertheless, all the papers clearly identified the ultimate goal of the study as identifying mechanisms to prevent MVCs among teen drivers and all trials collected MVC data and reported it, in some fashion. None of the interventions reduced teen drivers’ crash risk. We posit that this state of affairs is due to: 1) the lack of individual-level interventions mature enough to evaluate coupled with the quality of evidence directing the development of such interventions; 2) the small number of randomised controlled trials conducted to evaluate available interventions; 3) the low statistical power of these trials; and related to this, 4) the practice of combining crashes with other outcomes.

Intervention Development

Most interventions made contact with some theoretical framework or behaviour change theory, but others, mainly the classroom-based programs, were purely “knowledge-based.” To address this issue intervention developers could consider using the Behaviour Change Wheel.44 This tool supports intervention development and evaluation by distilling and harmonizing key components of prevailing behaviour change frameworks into the categories of capability, opportunity, and motivation. This approach makes the frameworks more accessible and promotes their correct application. Driver specific theories may also prove useful to guide intervention development. General theories of driver behaviour, not specific to novices, have been around for some time and tend to focus on the intersections among perceptions of task demands, risk appraisals and driver capabilities.45 These theories did not informed any of the interventions or training programs identified in this review. More recently, the Phase Transition Framework (PTF) is a dynamical systems account of driver behavioural development that posits driver behaviour is emergently generated, between and within-person data aggregation can mask change processes, detecting phase changes is important for future research, and that such phase changes in crash risk can be induced by the acquisition of superior cognitive and behavioural strategies to reduce risk.46,47 Theoretical, logical, and computational tests of the PTF model applied to secondary data have been encouraging,46 but prospective experimental studies are essential to properly interrogate this theory further.48

A more intractable problem is improving the quality of evidence on which interventions are based. In the context of HARKing, it is not difficult to find a null hypothesis to reject if one looks long enough. For a given published study, it is difficult to tell how many a priori or post hoc hypotheses were tested and not reported, which makes this is a really challenging problem. Pre-registration can address selective reporting of a priori hypotheses but not the selective reporting of post hoc hypothesis testing. This issue means that there are likely many null hypotheses that were unable to be rejected, but we do not know what they are, and that the findings that are published are not generalisable. The implication of this situation is that intervention developers may be chasing “noise” by targeting risk and protective factors where there is no true association between those factors and MVCs in the population, or the association is significantly smaller than reported. Interventions targeting such factors will fail when powered to detect previously reported effect sizes as these are likely over estimates of the true associations. In applied contexts, policy decisions or programmes that may appear on the surface to be “evidence-based” or “evidence-informed”, may not be. This can cause wasted resources and distrust of scientists when no positive outcomes are realised.

Several solutions have been proposed to remedy this situation including study protocol pre-registration (e.g., clinicaltrials.gov) and pre-registered reports where journal articles are reviewed prior to their results being known. Despite these efforts, there are generational barriers to culture change, in part, because some questionable research practices are normative.49,50 From the 1970s to the early 2000s these practices, particularly HARKing, were encouraged.21,51 Looking ahead, open science and rigour and reproducibility practices are now being introduced into higher-education curricula.52 However, as with other fields, these issues are affecting young driver research.53,54 Publication bias driven by disinterest in “null” results, HARKing, and P-value hacking will mis-direct the field towards targeting psychological and behavioural constructs that are not causally or meaningfully predictive of MVCs. This issue must be addressed through the adoption of rigor and reproducibility practices, norms, and values.

Trial Design

Acknowledging that MVCs are rare events with many different causal antecedents, concerns about low statistical power likely led to the practice of combining MVC data with other outcome measures (e.g., near crashes, tickets). We recognise the evidence that near crashes can serve as surrogates for crashes with some caveats55 and we are not advocating for dispensing with studies that use near crashes or hard breaking as outcomes, but rather to complement them with properly powered prospective experiments with MVCs as an endpoint. Combing real MVCs with other outcomes erodes precision and makes generalisation difficult. Innovative and collaborative approaches from adjacent fields (e.g., the NeOProM protocol)56 could inform how young driver researchers join-up to address the issue of needing a sufficient number of real crash events to detect meaningful intervention effects.

Finally, it is increasingly accepted that multiple interacting factors within drivers (e.g., state and trait factors) and between drivers and their environment (e.g., inexperience and weather) combine non-linearly to increase crash likelihood.47 One conceptual model used to describe this understanding is a “safe system approach.”57 The adoption of a safe system conceptual model aligns with the increased use of complex systems epistemologies and methodologies. Yet, we lack an understanding of how individual-level behavioural interventions could be deployed within a wider complex system of injury prevention.46,58 Concerningly, we also found no interventions for young drivers in low or middle-income countries consistent with other reviews.59

Conclusion

Systematic reviews of injury prevention interventions for children and adolescents on other topics have identified evidence of effectiveness for individual-level approaches for sports injuries14 and dog bites,60 but not for cycling injuries.61 It is possible the complexity of the traffic environment, as stated above, presents a unique challenge. Overcoming this challenge requires overcoming the MVC “power-problem” through collaboration and adoption of complex systems theories and methods.46,47,58 Robust investigation of individual-level interventions to reduce young drivers’ crash risk is crucial; however, GDL remains the best approach for reducing young drivers’ crash risk.
