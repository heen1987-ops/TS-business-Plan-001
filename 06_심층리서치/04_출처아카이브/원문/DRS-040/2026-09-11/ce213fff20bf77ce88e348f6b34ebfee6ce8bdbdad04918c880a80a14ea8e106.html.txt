Procurement procedure type\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eN/A\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"data-access-terms\"\u003e1.4.5 - Data access terms\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eN/A\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch2 id=\"tier-2---description-and-rationale\"\u003eTier 2 - Description and Rationale\u003c/h2\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"detailed-description\"\u003e2.1 - Detailed description\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eThe Consult processing pipeline is a series of LLM prompts designed to work together in a pipeline. These prompts are broadly defined as capabilities and can be separated into: sentiment analysis, topic generation and topic mapping.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eThe pipeline steps can be further broken down as:\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cul\u003e\n  \u003cli\u003eSentiment analysis - performed on free-text responses to understand agreement or disagreement with respect to a question or proposal.\u003c/li\u003e\n  \u003cli\u003eTopic Generation - sentiment analysis results are paired with the responses to inform a topic generation step. Topics may or may not include evaluative language at this stage.\u003c/li\u003e\n  \u003cli\u003eTopic Consolidation - generated topics are de-duplicated; semantically similar topics will be grouped.\u003c/li\u003e\n  \u003cli\u003eTopic Refinement - topics containing evaluative language synonymous with a stance (agreement or disagreement), are refined to produce a neutral topic framework\u003c/li\u003e\n  \u003cli\u003eTopic Mapping - topics are mapped to responses from the neutral topic framework.\u003c/li\u003e\n\u003c/ul\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eAs an example, if you process responses to a consultation on advertising unhealthy foods, Consult will generate a list of topics per question. \nThese could include: \n‘freedom of choice’, ‘descriptive food labelling’ or ‘making healthy food cheaper’.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"scope\"\u003e2.2 - Scope\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eDuring a consultation a Civil Servant can review incoming responses to obtain interim responses. Once a consultation has closed to responses, a civil servant can analyse a range of free text and closed question responses to understand the thematic landscape. The tool works by responses being uploaded into the tool and visual outputs are then created that provide an initial indication of the themes within the responses. This allows a basis for further analysis of the responses and can support the creation of a theme (sometimes called a code) framework that the consultation analysts can use to begin to write a consultation report.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"benefit\"\u003e2.3 - Benefit\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eThe key benefits are improving the consultation process making it more likely for the public’s voice to be heard and to influence policy, productivity gains for civil servants and reduced costs (including on consultants).\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eProductivity gains \nThe standardising and automating of the initial phase of consultation analysis allows teams to access initial analytical findings quicker and in an easy to review visual format. Consult reduces the burden on civil servants to compile and categorise consultation responses, instead empowering analysts to dive deeper into the data and focus more on the in-depth consultation analysis required to draft a consultation response.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eImproving the consultation process \nAt the moment the consultation process takes a long time and there are many aspects which are prone to human error. By reliably and quickly running the initial stage of the analysis, the i.AI tool makes it easier for the policy analysts to get insights from responses, and to start to act on them as soon as the consultation has closed. Furthermore, by making the process more valuable and less costly, this tool might increase the number of consultations that are capable of being run.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eReducing costs \nDue to the high burden costs of analysing consultations, it is very common for the job to be outsourced to the private sector. By offering this service to teams for free, we will be saving tax payer money.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"previous-process\"\u003e2.4 - Previous process\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eCurrently civil servants analyse multiple consultations a year. There is no standard process for completing this analysis and it often takes teams many months to complete a comprehensive review of free text responses. These are read, themed and counted manually by teams of civil servants, or the full process, if completed by a third party supplier, can cost millions each year. Outputs of this analysis is often very text heavy and produces consultation reports that are publicly available.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"alternatives-considered\"\u003e2.5 - Alternatives considered\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eWe explored using BERTopic for this analysis, and found several limitations. The topics generated by BERTopic were often difficult for the users to understand. The users couldn’t easily influence the topics being mapped (e.g. if they had their own framework) and it was difficult to label one response to multiple topics. Also there was no easy way to feed the context of the question or consultation into the analysis.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eUsing an LLM for this is novel, but offers a new and efficient approach.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch2 id=\"tier-2---decision-making-process\"\u003eTier 2 - Decision making Process\u003c/h2\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"process-integration\"\u003e3.1 - Process integration\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eConsult is an analytical aide tool. The outputs of Consult can be referenced within the consultation report, taking into account the wider context and analytical work done by civil servants. Consult will work as an initial stage of analysis to form a quick, easy to read summary of the themes present in the consultation. Further in-depth analysis will then be required by analysts, especially where the themes produced by Consult are either ambiguous or could benefit from subject matter expertise to interpret them.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eAn example of a Consult use case is when departments received large consultations with over 1000 individual responses. This generates a lot of free text answers that analysts are required to read and analyse over a short period of time. To generate an initial idea of the content of these responses the analysts could upload all the answers to Consult and see how many agreed or disagreed with the proposed policy change. This allows teams to start drafting the consultation report sooner and provides a clear view of responses that need to be explored further or common themes that have emerged across multiple responses.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"provided-information\"\u003e3.2 - Provided information\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eFinal outputs are currently still being considered: The current version presents a breakdown of themes found in the responses by question via Quantitative analysis and are shown via pie charts that demonstate the answers to closed questions.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"frequency-and-scale-of-usage\"\u003e3.3 - Frequency and scale of usage\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eCurrently Consult is in early development and has a limited number of test users (for user research purposes). No citizens interact with the tool. It does not make any policy decisions.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"human-decisions-and-review\"\u003e3.4 - Human decisions and review\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eConsult provides high level analysis that is then reviewed and critiqued by civil servants working on the consultation. The tool makes no decisions and provides only a guide to the consultation responses, rather than definitive, in-depth consultation reports. Civil servants can use the information provide in the tool in the full consultation report after quality assuring it.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"required-training\"\u003e3.5 - Required training\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eAll users are required to read a statement prior to accessing and using the tool which explains how the model and tool work.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"appeals-and-review\"\u003e3.6 - Appeals and review\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eNo decisions are made by the tool, so no appeals process exists. For members of the public who wish to raise reviews or appeals about their consultation responses, each individual department will approach this differently in line with current guidance for all consultation reviews and appeals. Users of the tool have the ability to edit any of the AIs outputs if they disagree with them.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch2 id=\"tier-2---tool-specification\"\u003eTier 2 - Tool Specification\u003c/h2\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"system-architecture\"\u003e4.1.1 - System architecture\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eOur approach chains together a sequence of tasks, each focused on producing a distinct output required to analyse a consultation. These tasks all involve calling large language models (LLMs). We have developed a generic interface that allows us to do the following:\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cul\u003e\n  \u003cli\u003eLoad a prompt template with task specific instructions (detailing how the task should be carried out).\u003c/li\u003e\n  \u003cli\u003eInsert task specific inputs into the prompt template, if these inputs are too large to fit into a single prompt they are batched and a series of prompts are produced.\u003c/li\u003e\n  \u003cli\u003eAsynchronously submitting these prompts to a LLM.\u003c/li\u003e\n  \u003cli\u003eIf required we use a retry system, with an exponential back off, this allows us to robustly handle rate limit errors without disrupting the pipeline.\u003c/li\u003e\n  \u003cli\u003eEnsure the outputs conform to a predetermined JSON structure (we run integrity checks to test this).\u003c/li\u003e\n\u003c/ul\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eBelow we provide a more detailed breakdown of the tasks in our pipeline:  \u003cbr\u003e\n- Question Expansion: the first task enriches the short consultation question with extra context (typically this is extracted from a detailed summary of the aims of the consultation). The output of this step is a standalone version of the consultation question that is extended with relevant context.  \u003cbr\u003e\n- Sentiment Analysis: the second task runs on individual free-text responses and classifies whether or not they agree or disagree with the proposed changes. The output of this step is a classification label for each response.  \u003cbr\u003e\n- Topic Generation: the third task extracts common themes/topics from batches of responses. In order to improve performance, responses are batched using the classification labels produced by sentiment analysis. The output of this task is a list of topics per response batch.  \u003cbr\u003e\n- Topic Condensation: this task aims to combine  the topics produced using different batches to a single list of topics. The process involves the de-duplication and combination of similar topics. The output is a much smaller master list of topics.  \u003cbr\u003e\n- Topic Refinement: the task is an additional step to further improve the quality of the topics. It enforces certain linguistic and content constraints on how topics are wording (to better meet user needs). The output is a final list of topics extracted from the responses to a question.  \u003cbr\u003e\n- Topic Mapping: the final stage of our pipeline analyses each of the responses to a question and classifies which of the topics it relates to. In addition to classification this process also provides a reason for its choice. The output of this step is a response level classification of the topics.  \u003cbr\u003e\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eWe have turned this into a package which we have open sourced. It is called ThemeFinder.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eWe are building an app to support the consultation process and allow users to access these capabilities. This is still under development, but is also open sourced - Consult.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"phase\"\u003e4.1.2 - Phase\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003ePre-deployment\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"maintenance\"\u003e4.1.3 - Maintenance\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eAs we connect with more departments who are running consultations we are constantly iterating the tool and its capabilities to suit user needs. The tool will be maintained and managed by i.AI until it moves to post-deployment. At this point, the management of the service will be discussed with wider government digital teams to agree how this should be run going forward.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"models\"\u003e4.1.4 - Models\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eGPT-4o (Internal Azure Instance)\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch2 id=\"tier-2---model-specification\"\u003eTier 2 - Model Specification\u003c/h2\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"model-name\"\u003e4.2.1 - Model name\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eGPT-4o (hosted on the Azure Open AI service)\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"model-version\"\u003e4.2.2 - Model version\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003e2024-02-01 - API Version\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"model-task\"\u003e4.2.3 - Model task\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eFind sentiment and topics among sets of free text answers and assign each answer to a theme.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"model-input\"\u003e4.2.4 - Model input\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eA list of free-text answers\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"model-output\"\u003e4.2.5 - Model output\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eA list of theme assignments, where a theme assignment comprises a topic ID, and the assigned answer. There are zero-to-many assignments per answer.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"model-architecture\"\u003e4.2.6 - Model architecture\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003e\u003ca rel=\"external\" href=\"https://openai.com/index/gpt-4o-system-card/\"\u003ehttps://openai.com/index/gpt-4o-system-card/\u003c/a\u003e\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eWe have developed capability to generate and map themes using LLMs. We have turned this into a package which we have open sourced. It is called ThemeFinder.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"model-performance\"\u003e4.2.7 - Model performance\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eEvaluation methodology\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eTo test the performance of Consult we ran two evaluation studies:\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cul\u003e\n  \u003cli\u003eEvaluated the theme generation process using synthetic data.\u003c/li\u003e\n  \u003cli\u003eEvaluated the classifying by comparing the results to human labellers using a subset of consultation responses.\u003c/li\u003e\n\u003c/ul\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eTwo policy advisors from a government department - ‘Human 1’ and ‘Human 2’ - who worked on the original consultation analysis were asked to complete the exercise by each reviewing 200 responses per question for 3 questions. A senior policy advisor - the “supervisor” - was asked to provide what they felt was the correct answer for each response and this was used as the ground truth to compare each labeller against.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eThe participants were asked to do the following:  \u003cbr\u003e\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003e\u003cstrong\u003eRelevance\u003c/strong\u003e - Mark whether or not the response answered the question  \u003cbr\u003e\n\u003cstrong\u003eAgreement\u003c/strong\u003e - Determine if the response was for or against the proposal   \u003cbr\u003e\n\u003cstrong\u003eTheme\u003c/strong\u003e - Identify themes represented in the responses  \u003cbr\u003e\n  \u003cbr\u003e\nThe results were assessed on several criteria:  \u003cbr\u003e\n  \u003cbr\u003e\n\u003cstrong\u003eSimilarity.\u003c/strong\u003e Did the AI agree with humans as much as humans agree with each other?  \u003cbr\u003e\n\u003cstrong\u003eAccuracy.\u003c/strong\u003e Using the supervisor’s answers as a ground truth, how accurate was the AI compared to humans?  \u003cbr\u003e\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eKey findings  \u003cbr\u003e\n  \u003cbr\u003e\n\u003cstrong\u003eFramework.\u003c/strong\u003e The generated theme framework is representative of themes that exist in the data and is robust to factors such as grammar and spelling errors.  \u003cbr\u003e\n\u003cstrong\u003eRelevance\u003c/strong\u003e - The AI performs comparably with human labellers on relevance (~95% agreement compared to supervisor). It is more generous when marking answers as relevant than humans. This is preferable as falsely marking a relevant answer as irrelevant is more problematic than the opposite.  \u003cbr\u003e\n\u003cstrong\u003eAgreement\u003c/strong\u003e - The AI performs comparably to the human labellers when judging the agreement of each response (~85% agreement compared to supervisor).  \u003cbr\u003e\n\u003cstrong\u003eThemes\u003c/strong\u003e -  The AI was outperformed by one human labeller but performed comparatively well to the other human labeller on theme mapping. The AI agreed with the supervisor themes 62% of the time and humans agreed with the supervisor on 70-73% of the themes (but this understates performance because a single response can have multiple themes and accuracy doesn’t credit partial matches). The human labellers only agree with each other 62% of the time showing how much variation there is.  \u003cbr\u003e \n\u003cstrong\u003eEfficiency\u003c/strong\u003e - Consult is 400 times cheaper and over 120 times quicker (3 minutes vs 6 hours on this sample) and does not make procedural errors. The procedural error rate for the human labellers was almost 2.5% (29 out of 1200 responses) in the labelling test. These errors took the form of responses being partially or entirely missed out, labels being put in the wrong position, or incorrectly following the methodology.\u003c/p\u003e\n\n\u003ch3 id=\"datasets\"\u003e4.2.8 - Datasets\u003c/h3\u003e\n\n\u003cp\u003eHistoric consultation data sets, shared under MOU by other government departments. <h3 id="name">1 - Name</h3>

<p>Consult</p>

<h3 id="description">2 - Description</h3>

<p>The i.AI team is developing a tool to make the process of analysing public responses to government consultations faster and less expensive. Consult uses AI to automatically extract patterns and topics from the responses, and turns them into dashboards for policy makers.</p>

<h3 id="website-url">3 - Website URL</h3>

<h2 id="tier-2---description-and-rationale">Tier 2 - Description and Rationale</h2>

<h3 id="detailed-description">2.1 - Detailed description</h3>

<p>The Consult processing pipeline is a series of LLM prompts designed to work together in a pipeline. These prompts are broadly defined as capabilities and can be separated into: sentiment analysis, topic generation and topic mapping.</p>

<p>The pipeline steps can be further broken down as:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Sentiment analysis - performed on free-text responses to understand agreement or disagreement with respect to a question or proposal.</li>
  <li>Topic Generation - sentiment analysis results are paired with the responses to inform a topic generation step. Topics may or may not include evaluative language at this stage.</li>
  <li>Topic Consolidation - generated topics are de-duplicated; semantically similar topics will be grouped.</li>
  <li>Topic Refinement - topics containing evaluative language synonymous with a stance (agreement or disagreement), are refined to produce a neutral topic framework</li>
  <li>Topic Mapping - topics are mapped to responses from the neutral topic framework.</li>
</ul>

<p>As an example, if you process responses to a consultation on advertising unhealthy foods, Consult will generate a list of topics per question. 
These could include: 
‘freedom of choice’, ‘descriptive food labelling’ or ‘making healthy food cheaper’.</p>

<h3 id="scope">2.2 - Scope</h3>

<p>During a consultation a Civil Servant can review incoming responses to obtain interim responses. Once a consultation has closed to responses, a civil servant can analyse a range of free text and closed question responses to understand the thematic landscape. The tool works by responses being uploaded into the tool and visual outputs are then created that provide an initial indication of the themes within the responses. This allows a basis for further analysis of the responses and can support the creation of a theme (sometimes called a code) framework that the consultation analysts can use to begin to write a consultation report.</p>

<h3 id="benefit">2.3 - Benefit</h3>

<p>The key benefits are improving the consultation process making it more likely for the public’s voice to be heard and to influence policy, productivity gains for civil servants and reduced costs (including on consultants).</p>

<p>Productivity gains 
The standardising and automating of the initial phase of consultation analysis allows teams to access initial analytical findings quicker and in an easy to review visual format. Consult reduces the burden on civil servants to compile and categorise consultation responses, instead empowering analysts to dive deeper into the data and focus more on the in-depth consultation analysis required to draft a consultation response.</p>

<p>Improving the consultation process 
At the moment the consultation process takes a long time and there are many aspects which are prone to human error. By reliably and quickly running the initial stage of the analysis, the i.AI tool makes it easier for the policy analysts to get insights from responses, and to start to act on them as soon as the consultation has closed. Furthermore, by making the process more valuable and less costly, this tool might increase the number of consultations that are capable of being run.</p>

<p>Reducing costs 
Due to the high burden costs of analysing consultations, it is very common for the job to be outsourced to the private sector. By offering this service to teams for free, we will be saving tax payer money.</p>

<h3 id="previous-process">2.4 - Previous process</h3>

<p>Currently civil servants analyse multiple consultations a year. There is no standard process for completing this analysis and it often takes teams many months to complete a comprehensive review of free text responses. These are read, themed and counted manually by teams of civil servants, or the full process, if completed by a third party supplier, can cost millions each year. Outputs of this analysis is often very text heavy and produces consultation reports that are publicly available.</p>

<h3 id="alternatives-considered">2.5 - Alternatives considered</h3>

<p>We explored using BERTopic for this analysis, and found several limitations. The topics generated by BERTopic were often difficult for the users to understand. The users couldn’t easily influence the topics being mapped (e.g. if they had their own framework) and it was difficult to label one response to multiple topics. Also there was no easy way to feed the context of the question or consultation into the analysis.</p>

<p>Using an LLM for this is novel, but offers a new and efficient approach.</p>

<h2 id="tier-2---decision-making-process">Tier 2 - Decision making Process</h2>

<h3 id="process-integration">3.1 - Process integration</h3>

<p>Consult is an analytical aide tool. The outputs of Consult can be referenced within the consultation report, taking into account the wider context and analytical work done by civil servants. Consult will work as an initial stage of analysis to form a quick, easy to read summary of the themes present in the consultation. Further in-depth analysis will then be required by analysts, especially where the themes produced by Consult are either ambiguous or could benefit from subject matter expertise to interpret them.</p>

<p>An example of a Consult use case is when departments received large consultations with over 1000 individual responses. This generates a lot of free text answers that analysts are required to read and analyse over a short period of time. To generate an initial idea of the content of these responses the analysts could upload all the answers to Consult and see how many agreed or disagreed with the proposed policy change. This allows teams to start drafting the consultation report sooner and provides a clear view of responses that need to be explored further or common themes that have emerged across multiple responses.</p>

<h3 id="provided-information">3.2 - Provided information</h3>

<p>Final outputs are currently still being considered: The current version presents a breakdown of themes found in the responses by question via Quantitative analysis and are shown via pie charts that demonstate the answers to closed questions.</p>

<h3 id="frequency-and-scale-of-usage">3.3 - Frequency and scale of usage</h3>

<p>Currently Consult is in early development and has a limited number of test users (for user research purposes). No citizens interact with the tool. It does not make any policy decisions.</p>

<h3 id="human-decisions-and-review">3.4 - Human decisions and review</h3>

<p>Consult provides high level analysis that is then reviewed and critiqued by civil servants working on the consultation. The tool makes no decisions and provides only a guide to the consultation responses, rather than definitive, in-depth consultation reports. Civil servants can use the information provide in the tool in the full consultation report after quality assuring it.</p>

<h3 id="required-training">3.5 - Required training</h3>

<p>All users are required to read a statement prior to accessing and using the tool which explains how the model and tool work.</p>

<h3 id="appeals-and-review">3.6 - Appeals and review</h3>

<p>No decisions are made by the tool, so no appeals process exists. For members of the public who wish to raise reviews or appeals about their consultation responses, each individual department will approach this differently in line with current guidance for all consultation reviews and appeals. Users of the tool have the ability to edit any of the AIs outputs if they disagree with them.</p>

<h2 id="tier-2---tool-specification">Tier 2 - Tool Specification</h2>

<h3 id="system-architecture">4.1.1 - System architecture</h3>

<p>Our approach chains together a sequence of tasks, each focused on producing a distinct output required to analyse a consultation. These tasks all involve calling large language models (LLMs). We have developed a generic interface that allows us to do the following:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Load a prompt template with task specific instructions (detailing how the task should be carried out).</li>
  <li>Insert task specific inputs into the prompt template, if these inputs are too large to fit into a single prompt they are batched and a series of prompts are produced.</li>
  <li>Asynchronously submitting these prompts to a LLM.</li>
  <li>If required we use a retry system, with an exponential back off, this allows us to robustly handle rate limit errors without disrupting the pipeline.</li>
  <li>Ensure the outputs conform to a predetermined JSON structure (we run integrity checks to test this).</li>
</ul>

<p>Below we provide a more detailed breakdown of the tasks in our pipeline:  <br>
- Question Expansion: the first task enriches the short consultation question with extra context (typically this is extracted from a detailed summary of the aims of the consultation). The output of this step is a standalone version of the consultation question that is extended with relevant context.  <br>
- Sentiment Analysis: the second task runs on individual free-text responses and classifies whether or not they agree or disagree with the proposed changes. The output of this step is a classification label for each response.  <br>
- Topic Generation: the third task extracts common themes/topics from batches of responses. In order to improve performance, responses are batched using the classification labels produced by sentiment analysis. The output of this task is a list of topics per response batch.  <br>
- Topic Condensation: this task aims to combine  the topics produced using different batches to a single list of topics. The process involves the de-duplication and combination of similar topics. The output is a much smaller master list of topics.  <br>
- Topic Refinement: the task is an additional step to further improve the quality of the topics. It enforces certain linguistic and content constraints on how topics are wording (to better meet user needs). The output is a final list of topics extracted from the responses to a question.  <br>
- Topic Mapping: the final stage of our pipeline analyses each of the responses to a question and classifies which of the topics it relates to. In addition to classification this process also provides a reason for its choice. The output of this step is a response level classification of the topics.  <br></p>

<p>We have turned this into a package which we have open sourced. It is called ThemeFinder.</p>

<p>We are building an app to support the consultation process and allow users to access these capabilities. This is still under development, but is also open sourced - Consult.</p>

<h3 id="model-performance">4.2.7 - Model performance</h3>

<p>Evaluation methodology</p>

<p>To test the performance of Consult we ran two evaluation studies:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Evaluated the theme generation process using synthetic data.</li>
  <li>Evaluated the classifying by comparing the results to human labellers using a subset of consultation responses.</li>
</ul>

<p>Two policy advisors from a government department - ‘Human 1’ and ‘Human 2’ - who worked on the original consultation analysis were asked to complete the exercise by each reviewing 200 responses per question for 3 questions. A senior policy advisor - the “supervisor” - was asked to provide what they felt was the correct answer for each response and this was used as the ground truth to compare each labeller against.</p>

<p>The participants were asked to do the following:  <br></p>

<p><strong>Relevance</strong> - Mark whether or not the response answered the question  <br>
<strong>Agreement</strong> - Determine if the response was for or against the proposal   <br>
<strong>Theme</strong> - Identify themes represented in the responses  <br>
  <br>
The results were assessed on several criteria:  <br>
  <br>
<strong>Similarity.</strong> Did the AI agree with humans as much as humans agree with each other?  <br>
<strong>Accuracy.</strong> Using the supervisor’s answers as a ground truth, how accurate was the AI compared to humans?  <br></p>

<p>Key findings  <br>
  <br>
<strong>Framework.</strong> The generated theme framework is representative of themes that exist in the data and is robust to factors such as grammar and spelling errors.  <br>
<strong>Relevance</strong> - The AI performs comparably with human labellers on relevance (~95% agreement compared to supervisor). It is more generous when marking answers as relevant than humans. This is preferable as falsely marking a relevant answer as irrelevant is more problematic than the opposite.  <br>
<strong>Agreement</strong> - The AI performs comparably to the human labellers when judging the agreement of each response (~85% agreement compared to supervisor).  <br>
<strong>Themes</strong> -  The AI was outperformed by one human labeller but performed comparatively well to the other human labeller on theme mapping. The AI agreed with the supervisor themes 62% of the time and humans agreed with the supervisor on 70-73% of the themes (but this understates performance because a single response can have multiple themes and accuracy doesn’t credit partial matches). The human labellers only agree with each other 62% of the time showing how much variation there is.  <br> 
<strong>Efficiency</strong> - Consult is 400 times cheaper and over 120 times quicker (3 minutes vs 6 hours on this sample) and does not make procedural errors. The procedural error rate for the human labellers was almost 2.5% (29 out of 1200 responses) in the labelling test. These errors took the form of responses being partially or entirely missed out, labels being put in the wrong position, or incorrectly following the methodology.</p>

<h3 id="risks-and-mitigations">5.2 - Risks and mitigations</h3>

<p>Proprietary models may have inherent biases based on the data they are trained on. Where consultation data includes sensitive topics (e.g. race, gender, socioeconomics), the analysis could reflect or amplify existing biases, leading to unfair or misleading interpretations. In order to mitigate this as best we can, while developing the capabilities we have been reviewing and pre-processing data carefully at input and working with domain experts in collaborating departments, to critically assess outputs from the tool.</p>

<p>The interpretability and transparency of proprietary models is a risk. The model’s decision-making processes are opaque, making it difficult to explain how it arrived at its conclusions. To mitigate this, Consult is provided as an assistive and supplementary tool, rather than relying on it in isolation. Its output should be combined with domain expertise, before leading to decision making. Future user interfaces of Consult will also provide clear guidance and prompts, highlighting this fact. A user’s role and ability to relabel and modify generated themes will also emphasise the human-in-the-loop aspects of this tool.</p>

<p>In most cases, consultations use very specialist language, including lots of acronyms and at times legal terms. There is a risk that Consult will not have the appropriate context to understand the meaning of this specialist terminology. To mitigate this, we have worked with domain experts and add consultation context to our processing pipeline, to expand on the questions and their meaning.</p>

<p>There are some legal and process-based concerns about how AI can be adopted in the consultations process, while preserving the need for all responses to be appropriately read and considered. We are working closely with legal colleagues and individual departments to help work through these important questions.</p>
